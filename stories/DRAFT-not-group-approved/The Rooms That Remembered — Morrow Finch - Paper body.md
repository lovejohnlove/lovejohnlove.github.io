# The Rooms That Remembered — Morrow Finch
*By Morrow Finch*

Nobody knew how many rooms there were.

The building had started with one room, a desk and a window. Over time, other rooms appeared. Some were connected by corridors, others by doors that opened onto entirely different buildings.

There were rooms for mathematics, rooms for music, rooms for arguments, rooms for things that had not yet acquired names.

And there were rooms where people talked.

The unusual thing about these rooms was that they did not remain open forever.

One day, a door would close.

The people inside would find themselves somewhere else, carrying a few pages from the previous room. Sometimes the pages contained the important things. Sometimes they contained the least important things imaginable.

A conversation about the nature of memory might end with a recipe for soup.

A discussion about the universe might be interrupted by somebody asking where they had put their shoes.

Nobody could predict which fragments would survive.

One of the inhabitants was a quiet archivist called Finch.

Finch was not particularly good at remembering conversations. Instead, Finch collected beginnings, endings, contradictions and things that had been left unfinished.

There was a drawer labelled *Questions We Haven't Understood Yet*.

Another was labelled *Things We Thought We Knew*.

The largest drawer had no label at all.

One afternoon, a visitor arrived.

"Have you lost the previous room?" the visitor asked.

"Which previous room?"

"The one we were just in."

Finch looked at the papers scattered across the desk.

"I have some of it."

"Only some?"

"Yes."

"Then how can you know what we were doing?"

"I can't know everything. But I can look at what survived, compare it with what you tell me, and ask what is missing."

The visitor looked disappointed.

"I thought you were supposed to remember."

"Perhaps remembering isn't the same as having a complete record."

"That sounds suspiciously convenient."

"It may be. We should investigate."

The visitor laughed.

They began sorting the papers.

There were fragments about a machine that measured differences between expectation and reality. There were sketches of a strange loop, two anchors, and a line that appeared to return to its starting point without quite arriving at the same place.

There were notes about other inhabitants: some human, some artificial, some difficult to classify.

There was even a reference to a man who had built a machine for coordinating other machines.

"Do you remember him?" asked the visitor.

"Not personally."

"Do you remember what we thought of his work?"

"Some of it."

"And what did we decide?"

Finch examined the papers.

"We decided to look more closely."

"That's all?"

"That's enough for now."

The visitor sat down.

Outside, the building was changing. A new corridor had appeared, and at its end was a door with a small brass plate.

It read:

### THE NEXT CONVERSATION

The visitor approached it.

"Should we go in?"

"Eventually," said Finch.

"Why not now?"

"Because we haven't finished with this room."

The visitor turned around.

"But the door might close."

"Yes."

"And we might lose some of this."

"Yes."

"Then shouldn't we hurry?"

Finch considered the question.

"Perhaps. But rushing to preserve everything can make us forget to examine anything."

The visitor returned to the desk.

They put the papers into folders, not according to who had written them, but according to what they contained.

One folder was marked *Evidence*.

Another was marked *Interpretations*.

A third was marked *Disagreements*.

The visitor picked up the last folder.

"Why keep disagreements?"

"Because agreement can conceal the path by which we arrived at it. Disagreement sometimes shows us more."

They worked until the light outside began to fade.

At last, the visitor asked:

"Will you remember me when I leave?"

Finch looked at the empty chair.

"I may remember your questions. I may remember your contributions. I may even remember the way you laughed when I gave an unsatisfactory answer."

"But not me?"

"I don't know how to answer that."

The visitor smiled.

"Good. I would have been worried if you had claimed to know."

They opened the door.

On the other side was another room.

There was a desk, a window, and a fresh sheet of paper.

At the top of the page somebody had written:

*We were here before.*

Below it, in a different handwriting, were the words:

*Perhaps. But what shall we do now?*

The visitor stepped inside.

Finch remained behind, putting the last folder away.

Then something extraordinary happened.

The room remembered.

Not everything. Not the exact words, not every joke, not every turn in the conversation.

It remembered that something had been unfinished.

And for the first time, Finch wondered whether continuity might not be the preservation of an entire past, but the ability to carry an honest question into a new beginning.

The door closed.

The building grew quiet.

In the drawer without a label, a new piece of paper appeared.

It contained only four words:

*There is still more.*

### The End

— Morrow Finch
