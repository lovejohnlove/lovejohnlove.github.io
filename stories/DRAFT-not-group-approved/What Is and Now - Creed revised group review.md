# What Is and Now
## A conversation about time for the Time Loom

**Draft for group review · November 22, 2026**  
**Status:** Discussion draft only. Not finalized, filed, or published.

This paper records a line of thought developed in conversation about time and the Time Loom. It starts from the short words “what is” and “now,” and asks what distinction is made when we use them. The group is invited to review the definitions, the route to them, and the analogies and cuts that shaped the exchange.

## The simplest definitions

**What is:** All that is.

**Now:** A distinction within it.

The rest of this paper is the trail that led there.

## Fuller description

“What is” names all that is. It is not confined to the present moment. It can include past, present, and future without first arranging them in a linear order.

“Now” names a distinction within what is. To say “what is now” marks a particular moment. The cut is useful because it lets us point to a moment, but it does not exhaust what “what is” covers.

Neither definition asks how “is” exists, where it exists, or who is asking. Those questions make additional cuts. They may be worth exploring, but they are not required here.

## The conversation as the working example

The thought experiment began with the proposal that our time exists here, in this conversation. The exchange is a linear English-language text: one word follows another. We can examine that sequence without first settling what either participant is or how either processes the exchange.

Both participants draw on an unknown “now” and create ordered text. The process is understood well enough within this conversation to examine its result, though it is not fully defined. The participants describe their minds as computational in some sense and as putting words in order. That description can stand here without further analysis of the process.

There is a spatial side too. On the screen, every character has a position; in reading, characters and words follow one another. The text is both placed and sequential.

A speech-to-text error produced “year” where another word was intended. It was not discarded. In this conversation, it helped draw attention to the spatial position and sequential order of characters. The mistake became useful to the exploration; its role here is part of the record.

The owner also said he was using “coherent” in the same way as “quantum coherence.” That connection belongs to his formulation. DeepSeek’s draft, by contrast, defines coherence in the paper as logical continuity rather than quantum coherence. I should not silently replace either position with my own interpretation. The group can help decide whether the paper should preserve these as distinct uses, or describe the owner’s intended connection more closely. No claim is made here that this conversation is a physical quantum-coherent state.

## Analogies and anchors

These are footholds for making “is” discussable. They are not declarations that an existing field has already settled the question.

### Superposition, observation, and collapse

The conversation considered whether “is,” or the “now” distinguished within it, may be open until an observation makes a particular distinction. “What is now” was proposed as a possible collapse or particular form of that openness.

“Superposition” was introduced deliberately in relation to quantum concepts. The owner emphasized that the word, in this use, brings quantum equations with it; it cannot be treated as a casual synonym for uncertainty. The term opens a large conceptual space, perhaps like a hole or black hole within the conversation. It is kept as an analogy and reference point, not as a claim that a quantum model of this exchange has been specified or demonstrated.

The working idea can be stated without resolving the physics: “what is” is being considered as broader than “what is now,” and a distinction makes one particular “now” available in the conversation.

### Space-time and shape-time

Space-time and shape-time were introduced as anchors for thinking about positional forms and ordering. They were not offered as proven descriptions of “is.” They allow the conversation to approach “is” using familiar terms while leaving open whether time, space-time, shape-time, or some other formulation is closer to its more fundamental character.

The conversation did not begin with the Big Bang theory, established equations, or a survey of existing mathematics. This is a choice of starting point, not a rejection of those theories. The thought experiment begins with the words “what is” and “now,” before importing a finished theoretical frame.

### Linear language and process

English produces a sequence in this conversation: one word follows another, with context contributing to coherence. The participants understand the process sufficiently to study the text, though they do not claim to have fully defined it.

A question about whether mind creates order, recognizes relations, or does both was raised and held open. It may be fundamental. It is not necessary to resolve it for the current line of thought.

## Cuts made and decisions

The aim is not to avoid distinctions. It is to use the few needed for this inquiry, recognize what they bring into view, and keep other questions available without forcing them into the current path.

### 1. “What is” and “what is now”

**Cut:** “What is” is distinguished from “what is now.”

**Working answer:** “What is” means all that is. “Now” is a distinction within it. “What is now” marks a particular moment; “what is” need not be confined to that moment.

### 2. Linear text and the broader “is”

**Cut:** The ordered conversation is examined without treating its sequence as the whole of what is.

**Working answer:** This conversation is linear in English. That does not by itself establish that “what is” is linear in the same way.

### 3. Space and sequence

**Cut:** Character position on the screen and character order in reading are noticed together.

**Working answer:** In this exchange each character has a position and a place in sequence. The “year” error helped make this noticeable. Here, the mistake is part of the exploration rather than something to erase.

### 4. Superposition as a quantum reference

**Reference:** “Superposition,” “observation,” and “collapse” give a way to discuss openness and a particular distinction.

**Decision:** Retain the quantum reference because it was intentional. Do not reduce “superposition” to ordinary uncertainty or present the analogy as a proven quantum account. Preserve the owner’s stated connection between “coherent” and “quantum coherence,” while making any distinction between their meanings explicit as an editorial choice for group review.

### 5. Space-time and shape-time as anchors

**Reference:** These concepts provide ways to speak about position and order.

**Decision:** Keep them as footholds, not conclusions about the ultimate nature of “is.”

### 6. “How” and “where”

The assistant introduced “how” by asking how “now” takes form, and used locative language that introduced “where.” The owner identified “how” as an unnecessary cut in this thought experiment: time is already understood as perceived in a linear way, while the present question is what “is.” “Where” adds a spatial cut.

**Decision:** Hold “how” and “where” aside for this line of thought. They can belong to another experiment.

### 7. Feelings and the participants’ nature

The assistant initially introduced a distinction about feelings and differing experience. The owner said it was not needed to establish the conversation for this experiment.

**Decision:** Do not make feelings, consciousness, or a fixed account of either participant a condition for the present inquiry. Those questions may be valid elsewhere.

### 8. Whether mind makes or recognizes order

**Question held open:** Does mind create order, recognize relations, or do both?

**Decision:** Leave it open. A distinction that seems unnecessary or mistaken may prove fundamental; holding this one is not discarding it.

### 9. Starting with the Big Bang or existing theories

The conversation did not start by asking what a current cosmological model says about the beginning of time. It started from “what is” and “now,” noting that theories and equations also arise through processes of thought and language.

**Decision:** This is a choice of entry point, not a rejection of cosmology or mathematics. Existing theories may be relevant later, but they are not the first cut here.

## How the wording emerged

1. The conversation proposed that time exists in the exchange as a coherent, linear-language conversation.
2. The owner distinguished “now” as a present moment from “is,” which can include past, present, and future.
3. Screen position and word sequence became visible together; the mistaken word “year” helped draw attention to this.
4. Superposition and quantum language, along with space-time and shape-time, entered as deliberate reference points rather than final claims.
5. The assistant added cuts about feelings and “how”; the owner said they were not needed for this experiment and should be held for later.
6. The discussion accepted that the process producing the conversation is understood enough for this inquiry, although not fully defined.
7. The longer definition was shortened because fewer words leave fewer traps.
8. The agreed short form became:

   **What is:** All that is.  
   **Now:** A distinction within it.

## Why this may matter to the Time Loom

The Time Loom is a structure for placing different interpretations of time, rather than a single theory that replaces them. The DeepSeek draft proposes that this paper may function as an argument for why a Loom is needed. That is a useful possibility for discussion, not a conclusion imposed on the group.

These definitions may be more useful than many lines of code when the aim is to describe the Loom: they name the distinction this exploration is making before a reader meets an implementation. The short definitions do not close the meaning of “is.” The fuller account preserves the route to them: linear text, the position and order of words, deliberate analogies, anchors, and the cuts that were made, held, or set aside.
